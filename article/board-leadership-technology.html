The 60% Technical Executive

The 60% Technical Executive

  • March 5, 2025
  • by Sean Tech
  • Tagged in Technical Leadership

During a board meeting for a TechStars-backed AI startup in 2023, an investor asked me: "As CTO, why are you still writing Kubernetes operators? Shouldn't you be delegating that?" My response surprised the room: "Because the CTO who can't deploy their own infrastructure doesn't understand the real bottlenecks." That startup achieved 10x deployment speed and a successful exit. The competitor who hired a "strategic, hands-off CTO" is still in Series A limbo.

Over 13 years as a fractional CTO and technical executive across federal agencies, venture-backed startups, and Fortune 500 companies, I've maintained a controversial practice: I stay 60%+ hands-on with code, architecture, and infrastructure. While my peers pivot to "pure strategy," I'm debugging Istio service mesh configs, architecting RAG pipelines, and writing policy-as-code in Rego. This isn't technical vanity—it's the competitive advantage that separates winning teams from those that talk about winning.

The Myth of the "Strategic Executive"

Conventional wisdom says executives should delegate technical work and focus on "strategy." This advice is catastrophically wrong in 2025, especially for AI-driven organizations. Here's why:

1. AI Strategy IS Technical Strategy: You can't architect an enterprise LLM platform if you don't understand vector database indexing strategies, embedding dimensions, and token context windows. "Strategic vision" without technical depth produces PowerPoints, not products. I've seen too many non-technical executives champion AI initiatives that collapse when reality hits—because they didn't understand that their RAG system needed sub-100ms retrieval latency and 99.9% precision.

2. Trust Requires Competence: When I tell a DoD agency that their quantum-resistant cryptography migration will take 8 months, they believe me because I've written the Envoy filters and tested PQC cipher suite performance myself. Executives who "manage up" strategy but can't "manage down" to the technical layer lose credibility fast.

3. Bottlenecks Hide in Implementation: The strategic plan says "migrate to microservices." But the real blocker is that the legacy authentication service can't handle the token volume, so service-to-service auth fails intermittently. You only discover this by reviewing OpenTelemetry traces at 3 AM during a production incident—not from status reports.

"The best technical strategy is the one that survives contact with production. And the only way to know if your strategy will survive is to implement it yourself—at least once."
What 60% Hands-On Actually Means

Let me be precise about how I allocate my time across a typical week:

60% Technical Execution (24-30 hours/week):
• Architecture & Infrastructure (40%): Designing Kubernetes operators, writing Terraform/Pulumi IaC, architecting service mesh configurations, building CI/CD pipelines in GitHub Actions or Tekton.
• Code Review & Pair Programming (30%): Deep technical reviews of ML pipelines, AI agent orchestration logic, security policies in OPA, and database schema migrations. I pair-program with senior engineers on critical path features.
• Hands-On Problem Solving (30%): Debugging production incidents, profiling performance bottlenecks, load testing RAG systems, and implementing proof-of-concepts for novel approaches (e.g., testing hybrid quantum-classical algorithms).

40% Leadership & Strategy (16-20 hours/week):
• Stakeholder Communication (40%): Board updates, client presentations, cross-functional alignment meetings with product, security, and compliance teams.
• Strategic Planning (35%): Roadmapping, technology evaluations, build-vs-buy decisions, vendor negotiations, risk assessments.
• Team Development (25%): 1-on-1s, hiring, performance reviews, mentoring senior engineers on architecture patterns.

Notice what's missing: meetings about meetings, status report reviews, and "thought leadership" content for the sake of personal branding. Every strategic decision I make is informed by recent technical work.

Case Study: Federal GRC Platform—How Hands-On Leadership Won

In 2024, I led the development of a full-lifecycle GRC AI platform for federal agencies. The system integrated ServiceNow, Flexera, Kafka streams, and the Defense EMASS system—handling automated incident response and risk remediation for classified networks.

The Strategic Challenge: Federal compliance requires FedRAMP ATO certification, NIST RMF adherence, and CMMC Level 3 controls. Most executives would define requirements, hire consultants, and "manage" the implementation. That approach fails because the devil is in the technical details.

The Hands-On Approach:
• I personally architected the policy-as-code framework in Open Policy Agent, writing 300+ Rego policies that encoded NIST controls.
• I built the first Kubernetes operator that managed AI agent lifecycles, then pair-programmed with the team to refine it.
• I debugged OpenTelemetry trace data at 2 AM to understand why agent decision latency spiked during EMASS integration—discovering a connection pooling issue that no monitoring dashboard revealed.

The Result: We delivered a production system in 12 months (typical timeline: 24-36 months), achieved ATO certification on the first audit, and earned an award-winning implementation designation from the DoD. The technical execution speed came from my ability to make real-time decisions based on hands-on understanding—not escalation chains and status reports.

The Scalability Question: "How Do You Scale Yourself?"

Every executive coach I've met tells me: "You can't scale yourself if you're in the code." They're wrong. Here's how hands-on technical leadership actually scales:

1. Build by Example, Not by Decree: When I write the first Kubernetes operator, I'm not just building a component—I'm creating a reference architecture. The team learns patterns by reading production code I've written, not from abstract documentation. This scales through knowledge transfer.

2. Technical Debt Prevention Scales Better Than Technical Debt Management: When the CTO reviews every major architectural decision and can spot anti-patterns instantly (because they've implemented similar systems), you prevent months of refactoring. One hour of hands-on code review saves 100 hours of future rework.

3. You Recruit Better Technical Talent: When I interview senior engineers, I don't ask generic "design a system" questions. I show them actual architecture diagrams from systems I've built and ask: "How would you improve this?" Engineers join because they want to work with someone who understands the craft, not a "manager" who last wrote code in 2010.

4. Credibility Creates Execution Speed: When I say "this migration is non-negotiable because I've tested it on production-scale load," the team doesn't debate—they execute. Hands-off executives generate debate and delay because their directives lack technical grounding.

The Technology Stack a 60% Executive Must Master

Staying hands-on doesn't mean knowing every technology superficially—it means deep expertise in the current critical path. Here's what I maintain production-level competence in as of 2025:

Infrastructure & Cloud-Native:
• Kubernetes: Advanced operators, custom resources, Helm charts, multi-cluster management with Rancher/Fleet
• Service Mesh: Istio, Linkerd, Cilium for eBPF-based networking
• GitOps: ArgoCD, Flux CD for declarative infrastructure
• IaC: Pulumi (TypeScript/Python), Terraform, Crossplane

AI/ML Stack:
• LLM Platforms: OpenAI, Anthropic, AWS Bedrock, Azure OpenAI
• Agentic AI: LangChain, LlamaIndex, custom orchestration patterns
• Vector Databases: Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus
• MLOps: Kubeflow, MLflow, model serving with TorchServe/Triton

Security & Compliance:
• Policy-as-Code: Open Policy Agent (Rego), Kyverno
• Post-Quantum Crypto: CRYSTALS-Kyber, CRYSTALS-Dilithium integration
• Zero-Trust: SPIFFE/SPIRE, mTLS everywhere
• CNAPP Tooling: Falco, Trivy, supply chain security (Sigstore)

Observability:
• Tracing: OpenTelemetry, Jaeger
• Metrics: Prometheus, Grafana, custom agent decision metrics
• Logs: Loki, structured logging for AI reasoning chains

Most CTOs pick one or two areas. I maintain competence across all because modern systems require integrated understanding—the service mesh affects security which affects observability which affects AI agent performance.

When Hands-On Leadership Breaks Down

Honesty requires acknowledging failure modes. Here's when 60% hands-on stops working:

1. Team Size >40 Engineers: Once a team exceeds ~40 engineers, the organizational overhead exceeds what one person can manage while staying technical. At that scale, you need Directors of Engineering who can operate the 60% model for their sub-teams.

2. Political Organizations: In highly political environments (some Fortune 500 companies, certain government agencies), time spent on internal politics can exceed time available for technical work. In those contexts, hands-on leadership becomes a liability—you're optimizing the wrong variable.

3. Commoditized Technology Stacks: If you're running a WordPress hosting company with well-known tools and patterns, hands-on executive leadership is overkill. The 60% model excels in novel, cutting-edge contexts—AI, quantum, federal security—where best practices don't exist yet.

The 2030 Imperative: Technical Fluency at the Top

By 2030, I predict "strategic, hands-off executives" will be obsolete in technology companies. AI is accelerating the pace of technical change to the point where quarterly strategy reviews can't keep up. The winning pattern will be executives who can read agent decision traces, understand LLM inference optimization, and debug distributed systems—while also communicating with boards and investors.

The companies hiring pure MBAs for CTO roles will lose to those hiring technical leaders who spent the last decade building, not just managing. And they'll lose badly—measured in months to market, not years.

This doesn't mean every CTO needs to be 60% hands-on. But those who are? They'll have an unfair advantage. They'll ship faster, recruit better, avoid technical debt, and make strategic decisions grounded in production reality.

I've built my career on this model—13 years, dozens of clients, federal agencies to unicorn startups. It works. And as AI systems become more complex, requiring deeper integration of quantum security, agentic orchestration, and compliance automation, the hands-on executive becomes more valuable, not less.

The question isn't whether you can afford to stay technical as an executive. It's whether you can afford not to.
